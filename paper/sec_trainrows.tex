\subsection{Training choices}\label{sec:trainrows}
Rows T1--T4 retrain B2 and B5 (five seeds each) and evaluate them in the reference environment (bottom block of
Fig.~\ref{fig:ctrl}).

\finding{Training without a friction drop makes the unshielded policy worse} (T1). Trained on $r=1$ only, B2 cut its grip
further ($1.25\,mg$) and failed 24\% of test lifts (195 of 800, against 92 in the reference); B5 and the test-time shields
were unaffected.

\finding{Longer training made the unshielded policy safe on this test set} (T4). With 40 instead of 20 iterations, B2
failed only 4 of 800 lifts (all from one seed), at 3.7 points more grip than the reference B2 and still 6 points less than
B5. Part of B2's fragility in the main study is therefore a training-budget effect: with more episodes, the occasional $-10$
penalty for a slip at $r\in[1,1.2]$ pushed the policy back from the edge. Nothing certifies this, and T1 shows how easily the
lesson is unlearned when the training distribution omits the drop.

\finding{A grip-heavy reward separates training-time from test-time shielding} (T3). With $\lambda=10$, one object-weight of
grip costs as much as a second of lift time. B5 then traded speed for grip, lifting in 0.50\,s at $1.18\,mg$, while B4g, the
shield applied only at test time to the fast B2 policy, lifted in 0.40\,s at $1.30\,mg$. In the main study the two coincided
because speed dominated the reward; when grip matters, putting the shield in the training loop lets the policy find
commands that need little projection.

\finding{Wider action, faster lifts, and the lag again} (T2). Allowed to lift in 0.25\,s, every policy did so, at
$1.75$--$1.85\,mg$ of grip. Certified lifts then failed at $r\ge1.2$ (B5: 20 of 200 at $r=1.2$, 170 of 200 at $r=1.3$). The
diagnostic row T2L, with the lag removed, had no shielded failures at any $r$, and B2's failures fell from 338 to 19: at
0.25\,s the peak acceleration is 9.2\,m/s$^2$ and the lag's $a\,\Delta t$ term crosses the switching threshold, as in E7.
